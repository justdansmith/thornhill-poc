Turf for the yard: who can help,
what it costs, and the path through OUSD.

What we found on companies and foundations that fund school play surfaces, a soccer partner that brings an after-school program, how a parent-funded project gets approved at OUSD, the trade-offs we will be asked about, and the Oakland schools that already did it. Every item links to its source.

Who can help pay for it

No turf maker offers a ready-made school program; a discount has to be negotiated with an installer. Two money sources are live right now: Sprinturf's LEAP matching grant, which closes October 30, and the America SCORES Field of Dreams model, which brings an after-school program with the field. The Curry foundation's schoolyards go through OUSD by need, and Joaquin Miller, a hills school in our ZIP code, got one in 2025, so that ask belongs with Principal Scott and OUSD Facilities rather than on an application.

Deadline October 30

Sprinturf LEAP Grant

What they give: Matching grants up to $50,000 per award for synthetic turf projects; applicants must match the amount Sprinturf provides. Open to schools, local governments, and community organizations. A play area qualifies if synthetic turf is its primary surface. Projects must be construction-ready within six months of award and installed by December 31, 2027.

Fit for Thornhill: The only turf-maker grant found with an open window that accepts play areas. Winners are announced November 30, 2026. Whether Sprinturf takes a project this small, and whether volunteers can install, is not on the page.

Next step: Call Sprinturf this week, then ask Principal Scott whether the school or OUSD can be the applicant and what counts as the match.

Confidence high

Eat. Learn. Play. schoolyard builds (Stephen and Ayesha Curry), with KABOOM! and OUSD

What they give: Full schoolyard renovations at OUSD elementary schools, student-designed and built with volunteers. The foundation's pages show 25 or 26 schoolyards done (two pages disagree) and 28 planned by the end of 2026, with the stated aim of eventually reaching every public elementary schoolyard in Oakland. Melrose Leadership Academy's Maxwell campus got a turf field and shade structure in 2024. KABOOM! runs the partnership as its 25 in 5 program, and Kaiser Permanente put $2.5 million toward the 25-schoolyard effort in 2023.

Fit for Thornhill: There is no application. OUSD picks sites based on need, judged on the play structure's condition and how much heat-trapping asphalt the yard has. Hills schools are not excluded: Joaquin Miller Elementary, 5525 Ascot Drive in Thornhill's own 94611 ZIP, was the 20th build in August 2025. The contact page lists only a press email, and a Director of School Partnerships job posting says the next phase's selection rules are still being designed.

Next step: Principal Scott asks OUSD Facilities Planning and Management how Thornhill gets considered for the post-2026 phase, and whether a PFC-paid turf area now would move Thornhill down the list. Dads volunteer at a nearby build to meet the KABOOM! and foundation staff.

What they give: A build model that pairs a community, a funding partner, and KABOOM!, with the playspace built by community volunteers. The Bay Area is a listed eligible region. Franklin Elementary in Oakland was built in a week of volunteer construction ending June 12, 2021.

Fit for Thornhill: The one national program whose model expects exactly what Thunder Dads offer: labor. Surface type and award size are not stated.

The soccer route: a partner that brings a program

America SCORES Bay Area helps a school raise money for a turf field and runs its after-school soccer and poetry program there; Oakland Roots joined that exact setup at Brookfield Elementary in 2023, and six San Francisco schools opened SCORES fields this fall. The U.S. Soccer Foundation offers two different things: an after-school curriculum the JCC could run, and a hard-court mini-pitch on the blacktop. The JCC aftercare is a partner in every version.

What they give: A soccer, poetry, and service-learning after-school program that partners with after-school providers, public schools, and districts, plus a Field of Dreams track: SCORES works with site leadership to raise funds for a turf field at the school, at about $10 per square foot for materials and labor, and has helped sites raise all or some of the money. The path through OUSD

OUSD's gifts policy (Board Policy 3290) decides the paperwork

Any installation becomes district property. The Deputy Chief of Facilities, Planning and Management may accept a gift of up to $50,000 per year without a prior Board vote, with Board ratification within 60 days; larger gifts go to the Board before acceptance. Gifts that create a program the Board cannot sustain, or undesirable or excessive costs, are rejected, and gifts carrying legal, financial, or program obligations get Legal Department review first. Online or crowdfunding campaigns need written prior approval from the Superintendent or designee. Policy text: open the 3000 series on the policy portal, then BP 3290. The Facilities page itself offers permit requests and work orders but no donated-project intake, so the ask goes by email.

Confidence high
boepublic.ousd.org/Policies.aspxwww.ousd.org/facilities-planning-management

Living Schoolyards is OUSD's own yard program, and it takes community proposals

The policy passed in 2019 (BP 7110.1) and the program sits in Facilities Planning and Management: assessment with the Joyful Schools facilities rubric, planning with community engagement, then construction. OUSD reports 22 projects and 473 trees, and says community members can volunteer on most builds. The 2023 Green Schoolyards America case study describes a second path beside district-led renovations: smaller community-initiated projects where champions write project proposals to the district, supported by an internal Garden Council. OUSD has also won $8 million in CAL FIRE grants for 2 implementation and 14 planning sites. There is no form; the main line is (510) 879-8000.

State Architect review, the labor agreement, and volunteer labor

The Division of the State Architect publishes IR A-22, Construction Projects and Items Exempt From DSA Review, plus bulletins on green schoolyards and playground surfacing; read A-22 before any design is drawn. OUSD's 2021 Project Labor Agreement covers work paid from county facilities funds, Measures A, B, J, and Y, and Proposition 39; whether a gift-funded project falls under it is not stated on the page. California Labor Code 1720.4, as applied in a 2014 state labor decision, exempts true volunteers from prevailing wage when they work for a public agency or 501(c)(3) without pay and are not also employed by a paid contractor on the same job. Get OUSD Facilities' answers on all three in writing before an installer quote is signed.

What to walk in knowing

The objections we will hear, with the documents behind them, so the proposal answers them first.

Health and heat: the evidence is split, and heat is the firm part

California's OEHHA final synthetic turf study (March 2026) found no significant health risk from chemicals in crumb rubber infill, confirmed elevated surface temperatures, and did not assess PFAS in the plastic blades or physical hazards. Mount Sinai's Children's Environmental Health Center (May 2026) strongly discourages artificial turf where children play and recommends natural grass, citing PFAS, microplastics, and surface temperatures over 160 degrees Fahrenheit on recycled rubber. The federal EPA and CDC research (2019 and 2024) found exposures likely limited and says it is not a risk assessment. Expect the 160-degree number at the first parent meeting.

California rules and PFAS claims

SB 676 (2023) lets cities and counties ban synthetic turf on residential property; the bill to ban PFAS turf at schools (AB 1423) was vetoed the same year, and no newer state school rule turned up (one search; the legislature's site blocked fetches). Purchase Green and SYNLawn Bay Area advertise PFAS-free products without naming third-party tests; Act Global says it will not claim PFAS-free for a product without independent testing. Ask any installer for the test report, not the label.

Who pays in year eight

A UMass Lowell cost analysis (2016, revised 2024) finds synthetic fields carry a higher life-cycle cost than natural grass, assumes surface replacement around year eight, and puts removal and disposal at $1.75 to $7.80 per square foot for full-size fields. Board Policy 3290 rejects gifts with excessive ongoing costs, so the proposal should name who maintains and replaces the surface.

Equity optics in Oakland

Think College Now, a flatland OUSD school, says its PTA raises under $2,000 a year while hills PTAs raise $250,000 to $400,000. The Oakland Ed Fund reported in 2020 that 15 OUSD PTAs raise $5 million a year combined, and that Equity Allies for OUSD raised over $100,000 from 230 donors for twenty $5,000 grants to the most stressed schools. OUSD's Living Schoolyards policy names Prioritize Equity as a goal. A premium surface bought by one hills PFC invites the widening-the-gap critique unless it runs through the district program or pairs with a gift to the Equity Fund.

Greening money is large, and only districts can apply for it

California's Green Schoolyard program was $117 million (2023), with implementation grants of $500,000 to $30 million capped at $2.5 million per campus; the 2024 round was $30.8 million and open only to school and community college districts. Green Schoolyards America says no state urban forestry funding is open right now, and PTAs were never eligible applicants. OUSD already holds $8 million of this money. The realistic move is to get Thornhill named in the district's next application, which means asking the Living Schoolyards staff, not writing a grant.

Parent pushback is a real risk

At Crocker Riverside Elementary in Sacramento (April 2026), parents objected to a turf project over heat and chemical exposure after the design was set; the district went ahead and said the time to revise had passed. Put the surface choice to Thornhill families before the quote, with the OEHHA and Mount Sinai documents linked.
